Build your package

$ millions

The amount is not what moves the odds. In 2,407 elections, the size of the ask shows no meaningful effect on yes share once date, type and community are accounted for, in any kind of district. The package is defined by which legal categories you include and when you run them. Amounts are here for planning and for the legal structure.

Texas law (Education Code 45.003, as amended by SB 30 in 2019) lets buildings, buses and sites share one proposition, and requires separate propositions for specified purposes such as technology, athletics and recreation, performing arts and staff housing. Every proposition must state that it is a property tax increase. Confirm your final structure with bond counsel.

Evidence

How the odds are built, and how far to trust them

The planner uses a regression model fitted to past Texas school bond elections. Here is what it learned, how well it predicts, and what it cannot see.

Pass rate by period and date

After 2019, November elections lost ground. May 2026 returned to the earlier pass rate.

Does party lean decide it?

Bonds in more Republican districts did pass less often. Most of that gap comes from home ownership, income and age mix. With those accounted for, party lean moves yes share by only about half a point for every 10 points of Republican share.

Election climate: how far each election ran from the model

Positive means voters were friendlier than the model expected. Over 18 election cycles since 2017 the gap rarely passed 3 points, and the last cycle did not predict the next. November 2026 ran about 13 points below, using 18 propositions in 9 districts, with the Irving, McKinney and Channelview vote shares estimated from reported ranges. It is the largest gap in the series.

How well it predicts

Tested on districts the model had never seen. Brier score measures probability accuracy, lower is better; guessing the average scores 0.223.

Live test on November 2026

Five propositions decided after the model was built, scored with history through 2025.

The model ranked the winners above the losers, but expected the three defeated propositions to land near 50 percent when they drew 40 to 42. November 2026 was a harsh year, which is why the climate setting exists.

What it cannot tell you

About 12 percent of the variation in yes share is explained by what can be measured from outside a district. The rest is local: trust, the plan, the campaign and recent history.

Districts choose their own election dates, so the date effect is an association. Party lean is included, but it carries almost no weight on its own.

The range shown reflects uncertainty in the model fit. The odds themselves already include the natural spread of results around the expected yes share.

Survey guide

Using a community survey alongside the model

A survey from a firm like Baselice and Associates measures your own electorate. The model measures what happened in similar districts. Used together they are stronger than either one.

  1. Test the real ballot language. Ask about each proposition with the required statement that it is a property tax increase. Surveys that asked about a tax neutral bond overstated support by about 30 points in the public cases we found.
  2. Ask about every proposition separately. Athletics and performing arts usually score below buildings. Separate numbers show which items put the package at risk.
  3. Sample the voters of your election type. A November electorate is larger and less engaged than a May electorate. A survey of all residents is not a survey of voters.
  4. Use the initial ballot as the baseline. The informed ballot shows how much persuasion could move people, not what will happen. The planner treats it as an upside scenario with a haircut.
  5. Field it close to the election. Support moves in the last weeks, and a survey taken in June says little about a November result.
  6. Enter the results in the planner. Give the percent for and against on the initial ballot, the number of respondents and the wording tested. The planner blends them with the model and shows how far the survey moved the odds.

How the blend works

The survey result is treated as a noisy and possibly biased reading of the electorate. The model gives a prior for the expected yes share and its uncertainty. The survey, with its error of about 9 points plus sampling error, pulls the estimate part of the way. The more respondents and the closer the wording to the real ballot, the more weight it gets. Hypothetical wording is discounted by 28 points before blending.

The error and the discount come from fewer than ten public survey and vote pairs. As more districts publish surveys, they can be refitted. If your district can share a survey and its result, it improves the tool for everyone.

About

Why this exists

Texas districts spend months building a bond package and then learn how voters feel on election night. This tool is meant to move that learning earlier.

What it is

A planning aid that estimates the chance a school bond proposition passes, from the date, the category, the community and the history of the district, adjusted for the election climate and any survey.

It runs alongside a survey firm, not instead of one, and it is not a guarantee.

Data

Texas Bond Review Board local bond election results from 2006 to 2025, Texas Legislative Council precinct returns for party lean, Census 2020 and the American Community Survey, and unofficial county and district results for November 2026.

Results for November 2026 are unofficial until canvassed, and the state does not require districts to report them until August 7, 2027.

Limits

The model explains about 12 percent of the variation in yes share. The rest is local: the plan, the campaign, trust and timing. Districts choose their own election dates, so the date effect is an association.
